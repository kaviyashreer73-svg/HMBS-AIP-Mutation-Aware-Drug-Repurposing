Mutation-Aware Computational Drug Repurposing for Acute Intermittent Porphyria

Overview:
This project investigates potential pharmacological-chaperone candidates for Acute Intermittent Porphyria (AIP) by studying the human hydroxymethylbilane synthase (HMBS) protein and selected disease-associated variants.
Rather than performing a conventional molecular docking study against a single protein structure, this project follows a mutation-aware computational workflow comparing predicted compound interactions between wild-type HMBS and selected AIP associated variants.
The study integrates protein structure analysis, binding-pocket characterization, compound screening, druglikeness and ADMET prediction, molecular docking, protein ligand interaction analysis, and biological pathway analysis.

Research Question:
How do selected disease-associated HMBS variants influence the predicted binding and interaction profiles of potential pharmacological chaperone candidates compared with wild-type HMBS?

Background:
Acute Intermittent Porphyria is associated with pathogenic variants in the HMBS gene, which encodes hydroxymethylbilane synthase, an enzyme involved in heme biosynthesis.
Reduced or altered HMBS function can contribute to the accumulation of porphyrin precursors associated with AIP.
Previous research has investigated HMBS pharmacological chaperones and identified compounds capable of interacting with the enzyme. This project therefore focuses on a different computational question:

Project Objectives:
1. Study the biological role of HMBS in heme biosynthesis and AIP.
2. Review existing research on HMBS mutations and pharmacological chaperones.
3. Select biologically relevant AIP associated HMBS variants.
4. Compare wild type and mutant HMBS structures.
5. Characterize potential binding pockets using CASTp.
6. Develop a small library of potential drug repurposing candidates.
7. Evaluate drug likeness and ADMET properties.
8. Perform molecular docking against wild-type and mutant HMBS structures.
9. Compare protein–ligand interaction patterns across variants.
10. Use protein interaction and pathway analysis to provide biological context.
11. Prioritize computational candidates for further experimental investigation.

Important Note:
The computational results can provide hypotheses regarding:
* predicted binding
* structural differences
* protein–ligand interactions
* drug-likeness
* ADMET properties
* candidate prioritization

However, computational docking and ADMET prediction **cannot prove actual HMBS binding, enzyme stabilization, increased HMBS activity, clinical effectiveness, or safety in AIP patients**.

The prioritized compounds should therefore be considered **computational hypotheses requiring experimental validation**.


Expected Outcome:
The final outcome will be a mutation-aware computational workflow for prioritizing potential compounds for further investigation as HMBS pharmacological-chaperone candidates.
The project will emphasize differences between wild-type and disease-associated HMBS variants rather than simply selecting compounds based on docking score alone.
